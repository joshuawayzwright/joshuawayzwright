# Five things your hourly rate has to cover (and why salary maths undercharges)

Here is a sum a lot of sole traders do in their head. You want to earn $80,000 a year before tax. There are 52 weeks in a year and a full week is 38 hours. So $80,000 ÷ 52 ÷ 38 comes to about $40 an hour.

It feels reasonable. It's also the figure that quietly leaves you short.

That's salary maths. It works for an employee because someone else covers the gaps. When you're a sole trader, the gaps are yours. Below are the five things your hourly rate has to cover, with one worked example so you can see how each one moves the number.

The example uses figures I picked for illustration: $80,000 a year before tax, 12% put aside for super, $12,000 a year in business costs, 6 weeks off, a 38-hour week, 60% of your time billable and a 15% buffer. Swap in your own numbers as you read.

## 1. Super

If you work for an employer, super gets paid on top of your wages. If you work for yourself, nobody does that for you. Whether you put money aside, and how much, is your call, but if you want a retirement fund, the money has to come out of what you charge.

In the example, I've used 12% of the $80,000 target:

$80,000 + 12% = $89,600

Spread over the same 1,976 hours (52 weeks × 38), that's about $45 an hour, up from $40.

The right super figure for your situation depends on your circumstances. Check the current rules with the ATO or a registered tax agent.

## 2. Business costs

An employee doesn't buy their own tools, fuel, phone plan, insurance or software. You do. Think about the vehicle, tools and consumables, phone, insurance, software subscriptions and anything else that has to be paid for before you earn a dollar.

In the example I've used $12,000 a year:

$89,600 + $12,000 = $101,600

Over 1,976 hours, that's about $51 an hour.

## 3. Time off

Holidays, public holidays and sick days don't pay when you're self-employed. The week you take off is a week with no income, so the weeks you do work have to carry it.

In the example, I've taken 6 weeks off. That leaves 46 working weeks:

46 weeks × 38 hours = 1,748 hours

$101,600 ÷ 1,748 hours is about $58 an hour.

## 4. Hours you can't bill

This is the one that surprises people most. Quoting, travelling between jobs, admin, invoicing, answering messages and finding the next job all take real time. None of it appears on an invoice.

In the example, only 60% of working hours are billable:

1,748 hours × 60% = about 1,049 billable hours

Now $101,600 ÷ 1,049 hours is about $97 an hour. That's the break-even rate: the figure where you earn exactly the $80,000 target (before tax), cover super and costs, and have nothing left over.

The reason the number jumps is simple. If only 60% of your time is billable, every billable hour has to pay for the other 40% as well.

## 5. A buffer

Break-even assumes every week goes to plan. Real weeks don't. Jobs get postponed, the weather turns, quotes go quiet and some invoices arrive late. A buffer is what stops one bad month from becoming a cashflow problem.

In the example I've added 15%:

$96.87 × 1.15 = $111.40, which I round up to $112 an hour.

The 15% is an illustration. Your own buffer might be bigger or smaller depending on how steady your work is.

## Putting it together

| Step | What changes | Rate per hour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salary maths | $80,000 ÷ 1,976 hours | about $40 |
| Add super | $89,600 | about $45 |
| Add business costs | $101,600 | about $51 |
| Take out time off | 1,748 hours | about $58 |
| Count only billable hours | 1,049 hours | about $97 |
| Add a 15% buffer | | about $112 |

Same $80,000 target, before tax. The hourly rate is nearly three times the salary maths figure. Nothing in the example is padding. Each line is a real cost that has to come from somewhere, and the only place it can come from is the rate you charge.

Two notes on what this doesn't do. It doesn't include income tax, so it isn't a take-home figure, and your own numbers will differ from mine. If you charge $55 an hour because that's what a mate charges, check which of the five items you've left out. That's usually where the gap is.

## What to do this week

Before you set your rates for the new year, take 20 minutes with a pen and paper and work through the five items using your own numbers:

1. Write down the before-tax income you want.
2. Add what you'll put aside for super.
3. Add a full year of business costs. Go through your bank statements, because memory undercounts.
4. Count the weeks you'll actually work, then estimate what share of those hours you really bill.
5. Add a buffer, and compare the result with what you charge now.

If the gap is big, you don't have to fix it overnight. Even knowing the number changes how you quote.
